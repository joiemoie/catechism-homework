Part VI: Class Discussion — The Crusades

Context from last class: We discussed how medieval Christians had a different relationship to knowledge than we do today. Through liturgy, repetition, and lived faith, they developed strong connatural knowledge (knowing through the heart and through virtue) and memoria (deep knowledge formed by constant repetition — prayers, Scripture, the liturgical calendar). Just as the Apollo astronauts, at the height of human technical achievement, still found themselves reaching for something transcendent, medieval Christians were driven by a hunger for God that shaped their entire world.

This hunger animated the Crusades. In 1009, the Fatimid Caliph Al-Hakim ordered the destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulcher in Jerusalem — the holiest site in Christendom, built over the place of Christ’s death and Resurrection. In 1071, the Byzantine Empire was crushed by the Seljuk Turks at the Battle of Manzikert, opening Anatolia to conquest and cutting off Christian pilgrims from the Holy Land. The Byzantine Emperor Alexios I appealed to Pope Urban II for help.

At the Council of Clermont in 1095, Pope Urban II called the First Crusade. Those who answered sewed a cross onto their arm as a public, binding vow. They received no pay — they had to fund the journey themselves, many selling their lands and possessions. Countless died before even reaching the Holy Land. The terms were stark: flee or abandon your vow = excommunication. Die in battle = a plenary indulgence — full remission of the temporal punishment for all sins.

The Crusaders marched through Anatolia. At the siege of Antioch in 1098, the Holy Lance — the spear believed to have pierced Christ’s side — was reportedly discovered, giving the army a surge of faith and courage. On July 15, 1099, they took Jerusalem.
